Week 1  ·  Introduction to Scientific Reasoning

Popper, Kuhn and Lakatos

How do we come to know the natural world, and by what standard is one account of it judged better than another? The extracts set for this week enter a defining twentieth-century debate on what separates science from its imitations — and on whether that line can be drawn at all.

01

Introduction

Lakatos writes in reply to both Popper and Kuhn. His essays are best read not as a position argued in isolation, but as one voice in a sustained exchange between the three over how scientific knowledge is best achieved and how it may be appraised.

You might reasonably ask what a dispute in the philosophy of science from the 1970s is doing in week 1 of a methods course. The reason is that judging the quality of evidence, and the accuracy of the claims drawn from it, is never merely a matter of finding technical faults in a model. It requires attention to how the research was set up in the first place.

A degenerating programme, in Lakatos's account, is one whose theories are fabricated after the event to accommodate facts already known, and then defended by adding qualifications each time the result fails to hold. It is a description of what it looks like to bend or cherry pick the facts to fit a narrative — a narrative that may be scientific, ideological, or both at once. Whether that has happened in a particular literature is exactly the kind of question you will be asked to judge, with data in front of you, later in the course.

Read it alongside Feynman's Cargo Cult Science, also set for this week. Lakatos gives you the problem in the abstract — what separates a progressive research programme from a degenerating one. Feynman gives you the same idea as a matter of personal conduct: report what might invalidate your result, publish whichever way it comes out, and remember that the first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and you are the easiest person to fool. .

The reader

These are the authors' own short statements, not their full works

Each piece in the reader is a lecture, a broadcast talk or an address in which the author set out his position for a general audience. Lakatos's Science and Pseudoscience, for instance, was written to be read aloud on the radio.

These pieces are published and properly citable in their own right, but consult the context of the full statement before you decide to use these in your own argument to avoid misunderstanding.

03

The Full Works

Additional bibliography. None of this is required reading. It is here so that you can see where each of these positions is developed at length, and so that anyone who wants to follow the argument properly knows where to go.

Start here if you read only one

  • Lakatos, I., & Musgrave, A. (eds.) (1970). Criticism and the Growth of Knowledge. Cambridge University Press. The proceedings of the 1965 London colloquium where the dispute actually happened. Contains Kuhn's "Logic of Discovery or Psychology of Research?", Popper's reply "Normal Science and its Dangers", and Lakatos's long essay "Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes". The three positions in one volume, arguing with each other directly.

Popper

  • Popper, K. R. (1959). The Logic of Scientific Discovery. Hutchinson. Originally published as Logik der Forschung, 1934. The falsifiability argument in full.
  • Popper, K. R. (1963). Conjectures and Refutations: The Growth of Scientific Knowledge. Routledge. More accessible than the Logic. The opening essay is the usual place to start.

Kuhn

  • Kuhn, T. S. (1962). The Structure of Scientific Revolutions. University of Chicago Press. The source of "paradigm" and "paradigm shift". The 1970 second edition adds a postscript replying to critics, including Lakatos.

Lakatos

  • Lakatos, I. (1978). The Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes: Philosophical Papers, Volume 1. Edited by J. Worrall & G. Currie. Cambridge University Press. Published after his death. The full statement of hard cores, protective belts, and progressive versus degenerating programmes. "Science and Pseudoscience" appears here as the introduction.
  • Lakatos, I. (1976). Proofs and Refutations: The Logic of Mathematical Discovery. Cambridge University Press. Not about the sciences, but the clearest demonstration of how he thought knowledge actually grows. Written as a classroom dialogue.

Feynman

  • Feynman, R. P. (1974). Cargo Cult Science. Engineering and Science, 37(7), 10–13. The Caltech commencement address, in the original.
  • Feynman, R. P. (1985). Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman! W. W. Norton. The address is reprinted as the closing chapter.

The fourth voice

  • Feyerabend, P. (1975). Against Method. New Left Books. Lakatos's friend and opponent, who concluded that no methodological rule has survived contact with the history of science. The two planned to publish a debate together; Lakatos died before it was written.
